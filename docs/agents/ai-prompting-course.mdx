## AI Novice vs AI Power User

It is now 2026 and prompting AI models is very different from when ChatGPT first came out in 2022. Using AI well is one of the most impactful skills you can develop. People who are not yet at the cutting edge of AI usage often end up with frustrating outputs.

### Comparison Table

| Behavior | AI Novice | AI Power User |
|---|---|---|
| **Question complexity** | Uses AI for simple Google-style searches (e.g., "Does Taco Bell still have the double decker taco?") | Asks hard questions and gives AI time to think deeply |
| **Context provided** | Sends a short prompt and hopes AI fills in the blanks | Uploads documents, screenshots, voice memos, and rich background context |
| **Feedback seeking** | Asks biased questions that hint at the desired answer | Asks neutral questions with no hint of the expected answer; uses rubrics |
| **Writing workflow** | Asks AI to jump directly into writing the final piece | Outlines first, critiques the outline, iterates several times before drafting |
| **Car buying example** | No upload | Uploads cost specs, quotes, insurance plans; asks for trade-off analysis |
| **Self-review example** | "Please write a good self-review to send to my boss" | Uploads project tracker screenshots, project docs, voice memo notes, then requests the review |
| **Business idea critique** | "I have a great business idea, mobile tie-dying. Critique it." | "Please analyze the following business idea objectively: mobile tie-dying. Use the rubric above." |

### Key Insight: Think of AI as a Smart New Hire

> Think of AI as a really smart, fresh college grad — highly motivated, but doesn't know much about you yet. A short prompt often doesn't give it enough information to answer accurately.

Power users almost have **empathy for the AI**: they ask themselves, *"Will this AI actually know enough about me to do a good job?"*

## How AI Gets Its Knowledge: Pre-Trained Knowledge

### How AI Learns

Just as humans learn to write by reading a lot, AI systems have learned patterns from reading **large amounts of text from the internet**. Understanding what's in that training data helps you predict when AI will be reliable and when to be more cautious.

### What AI Has Read

AI models are trained on many diverse sources:

| Source Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Social media & forums | Reddit, Quora |
| Books | Fiction, non-fiction, textbooks |
| Encyclopedias | Wikipedia |
| News websites | Mainstream and niche publications |
| Research articles | Academic and scientific papers |
| General web content | Blogs, documentation, government sites |

The total training corpus is estimated at **trillions to tens of trillions of words**.

### Knowledge Frequency = Reliability Rule of Thumb

The frequency of a topic on the internet gives you a rule of thumb for how reliable AI's response will be:

| Topic Type | Internet Frequency | AI Reliability |
|---|---|---|
| Cooking | Very high (universal human experience) | High |
| Celebrities & movies | Very high | High |
| Quasars (astrophysics) | Low (specialized) | Lower |
| Cantonese language content | &lt;0.1% of all internet content | Lower |
| Your company's secret proprietary data | Zero (not public) | Zero — AI has no knowledge of it |

### Pre-Trained Knowledge: What the Term Means

**Pre-trained knowledge** is a technical term referring to the first stage of AI training, where the model learns from massive internet data. It is simply what the AI has learned before any fine-tuning. You do not need to understand the mechanics deeply — just know that it represents the AI's "base knowledge."

### Surprising Capabilities from Pre-Training

- **Misspelled text**: AI handles typos remarkably well because it has been trained on text from the internet, which itself contains many typos and informal spelling.

```
"can you cook eggs in microwave?" (heavy typos)
→ AI understands this just as well as the correctly spelled version.
```

> **Practical tip**: Don't worry too much about fixing every typo before sending a prompt. The occasional typo does not significantly degrade AI output quality.

### Limitations

- AI reflects **misconceptions and outdated information** that exist in its training data.
- Your goal as a prompter is to minimize the impact of this by prompting well.

## Web Search and Real-Time Information

### The Knowledge Cutoff Problem

At some point, the builders of an AI model have to stop training. The AI's knowledge becomes **frozen in time** as of that cutoff date. The world moves on, but the pre-trained model does not.

**Example:**

> GPT-5.4 (OpenAI) had a knowledge cutoff of August 2025. The "6÷7" (pronounced "67") meme went viral *after* that cutoff date, so the model was unaware of it without web search.

### When Web Search is Triggered

| Query Type | Example | Trigger Method |
|---|---|---|
| Current events / recent memes | "What is the 67 meme from 2025?" | Automatic (AI detects temporal cue) |
| Location-specific information | "Find me a highly rated gym near Mountain View, CA" | Automatic |
| Niche or obscure information | "What is the Market Mountain cheese roll?" | Automatic |
| User-explicit request | "Please do a web search for this" | Manual |
| General knowledge / timeless facts | "Why do cats stare at walls?" | No web search needed |
| Historical knowledge | "What's on the Voyager 1 record?" | No web search needed |

### How Web Search Works Under the Hood

Web search in AI models is a **two-model, multi-step process**:

```
User
  │
  ▼
[User-Facing AI Model]  ←── You interact with this model only
  │
  │ (decides to call web search)
  ▼
[Assistant AI Model]
  │
  ├── Queries a search engine (similar to Google/Bing)
  ├── Scans and filters results
  ├── Downloads most relevant web pages
  └── Summarizes them
  │
  ▼
[User-Facing AI Model receives summaries]
  │
  ▼
Final answer generated for user
```

> **Important quirk**: The user-facing AI model has **not read the full web pages** it cites. It only sees summaries. This is why AI sometimes misinterprets or misattributes what a source actually says. Always double-check citations for high-stakes uses.

### Source Quality Problem

According to research, the most cited websites by AI models are:

1. Reddit
2. Wikipedia
3. YouTube
4. Google
5. Yelp

Many of these are not rigorously verified scientific sources. If you do not steer the model, it will tend to pull from **whatever is most available**, not what is most reliable.

### How to Get More Reliable Sources

```
❌ Less effective:
"How safe are gray market peptides?"

✅ More effective:
"How safe are gray market peptides? Use sources from official health organizations
such as the WHO, FDA, or European Medicines Agency, and prefer peer-reviewed studies."
```

### Outdated Web Pages

Web search can return pages that are **decades old**. In one example, a friend used AI to find running spots in Henderson, Nevada, and it recommended a school — sourced from a page over two decades old — that is no longer open to the public.

### AI Model vs. Web Search Engine: When to Use Which

| Use Case | Best Tool |
|---|---|
| Quickly scan multiple sources | Web search engine (Google, Bing) |
| Navigate to a specific website | Web search engine |
| Find a product to purchase (e.g., 2013 Honda Civic air filter) | Web search engine |
| Synthesize information from multiple sources | AI model with web search |
| Compare pros/cons across multiple perspectives | AI model with web search |
| Complex research with weighted conclusions | AI model — Deep Research mode |

## Deep Research

### What It Is

Deep Research is an **agentic AI mode** available in popular AI chat interfaces (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) that allows the AI to synthesize dozens or more sources and think at length to produce a deeply researched report.

> This is one of the most powerful and most underused AI capabilities.

### How It Works

```
User submits research prompt
  │
  ▼
AI formulates a research plan
  │
  ├── Issues many web searches simultaneously (parallel, not sequential)
  ├── Evaluates relevance of returned sources
  ├── Decides whether to do additional targeted searches
  └── Loops through: search → evaluate → search again (if needed)
  │
  ▼
AI synthesizes all downloaded pages into a comprehensive report
  │
  ▼
Report presented to user with citations
```

**Key advantage**: Deep Research can issue many web searches **at the same time**, making it much more efficient than manual sequential searching.

### Example Use Case: Halloween Haunted House Planning

**Prompt:**
```
Help me set up a haunted house in my front yard for Halloween.
I'm in Palo Alto, CA. My front yard is approximately 20x30 feet.
I want a family-friendly but spooky experience for kids aged 5-12.
Budget is $300.
```

The AI will:
1. Propose a research plan (which you can approve or edit)
2. Search for local permit requirements and Halloween ordinances
3. Search for fire safety guidelines
4. Search for decoration and prop ideas
5. Write a detailed sectioned report covering structural considerations, regulatory framework, safety, budget, and decoration ideas

### When to Use: Comparison Table

| Scenario | Tool |
|---|---|
| Quick single-fact lookup ("What's the weather in Dubai?") | Pre-trained knowledge or basic web search |
| Find a highly rated gym nearby | Basic web search (auto-triggered) |
| Impact of daily steps on long-term health (scientific synthesis) | Deep Research |
| How does weather affect tourism in Dubai? (multi-dimensional) | Deep Research |
| Complex business or investment analysis | Deep Research |

### Triggering Deep Research

Deep Research is almost always **explicitly triggered by the user** — either by selecting it in the UI or by specifying it in the prompt. AI will not run a deep research session and keep you waiting for minutes unless you ask for it.

### Summary: Three Information-Finding Modes

| Mode | Sources Downloaded | Freshness | Time to Answer | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-trained knowledge | None | Up to knowledge cutoff | Seconds | Facts, definitions, general knowledge well-represented on internet |
| Web search | Handful of sources | Up-to-date | Many seconds | Real-time info, location queries, niche topics |
| Deep Research | Dozens or more | Up-to-date | Many minutes or longer | Complex questions requiring synthesis of many sources |

## AI as a Thought Partner: Brainstorming

According to OpenAI data analyzing ChatGPT conversations, **about half of all ChatGPT chats are asking for writing and practical guidance**, and **~4% are creative ideation**. Brainstorming with AI is one of the highest-value use cases.

### Simple Option Generation

AI can generate large lists of ideas quickly. A classic creativity test asks people to name 200 uses for a brick. AI can do this easily. **Your role** is to evaluate and select from the generated options.

> Principle: In brainstorming, more ideas = better. Having AI generate a large idea pool for you to filter is a powerful strategy.

### Why Generic Prompts Yield Generic Results

AI was trained on internet text. There is far more text about common exercises (dumbbell curls, squats) than about unusual ones (cat-triggered micro-workouts). This means:

```
Probability of AI giving a response
        │
  High  │  ██████████████████
        │  ██████
        │  ██
  Low   │  █
        └────────────────────────────────────────
             Common sense      Highly unique/creative
             (bicep curls)     (cat-triggered workouts)
```

**To get more creative responses: provide more specific context.**

### Effective Brainstorming Workflow

```
Step 1: Provide rich context upfront
  │
  ▼
Step 2: Ask for 3–5 options (not just one answer)
  │
  ▼
Step 3: Give feedback on what you like / don't like
  │
  ▼
Step 4: Request 3–5 new options based on your feedback
  │
  ▼
Step 5: Repeat until you have a satisfying option
  │
  ▼
Step 6: Ask AI to flesh out the winning option(s) in detail
```

### Example: Debt Payoff Brainstorming

**Initial prompt with context:**
```
I have $1,100 of credit card debt at 19% interest, monthly minimum payments of $40.
I also have a student loan at 8% interest and a family loan of $900.
Give me 3–5 options for a debt payoff plan.
```

**AI responds with options:**
- Plan 1: Liquidity first — preserve cash
- Plan 2: Eliminate highest interest loan first (avalanche method)
- Plan 3: Prioritize paying family back first

**User provides feedback:**
```
I don't like option 1, it's too passive.
I like the 19% interest approach.
Oh, I forgot — I have $450 cash coming in and I'm also moving house soon.
Create 3 new plans incorporating this.
```

This feedback loop efficiently surfaces what context matters and what direction the AI should take.

### Summary: Brainstorming Best Practices

- Give as much **relevant context** as possible upfront (age, constraints, preferences, resources)
- Ask for **multiple options** (3–5), not a single recommendation
- Provide **specific feedback** on each option to guide the next iteration
- Repeat the feedback loop several times until satisfied
- Only then ask AI to **flesh out the details** of the best option

## Context: How It Works and Why It Matters

### Human vs. AI Working Memory

| | Humans | AI Models |
|---|---|---|
| Working memory capacity | ~7 items | Hundreds of thousands of words |
| Context window (leading models) | N/A | ~750,000 words (~first 4–5 Harry Potter books) |

Many people **drastically underestimate** how much context they can give an AI model.

### What's in the AI Context Window

When you interact with an AI model, the context contains:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  System Prompt                                           │
│  (Current date, model name, general instructions,       │
│   available tools and how to use them)                   │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  Your Uploaded Files                                     │
│  (PDFs, spreadsheets, images, documents)                 │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  Chat History                                            │
│  (All your prompts + all AI responses so far)            │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  Your Current Prompt                                     │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

All of this is used together to generate each response.

### Context Quality Principle

> Think about what information a **trusted expert advisor** would need in order to reason carefully and give you a great answer. Then provide that information.

**Example:**

```
❌ Low context:
"Pros and cons of studying physics versus zoology."
→ Generic answer

✅ High context:
"Pros and cons of studying physics versus zoology."
+ Upload: your career assessment results
+ Upload: your high school course schedule
→ Highly customized, relevant answer
```

### When to Start a New Conversation

If you switch to an **unrelated topic**, start a new chat. Previous context about your workout plan, for example, is irrelevant — and potentially distracting — when you ask about your mother's workout plan.

> Stale, irrelevant context can degrade response quality. Clear it out by starting fresh.

## AI Desktop Apps and Agentic File Management

### What These Apps Are

Applications like **Claude Cowork**, **Microsoft Copilot** (desktop), and **Google Antidote** can — with your permission — **agentically gather context from your computer**: finding, reading, and acting on files as needed.

### Example Use Case: Organizing a Research Folder

```
User: "Read through the files in my /research folder
       and propose a new organization based on what you find."

AI (agentically):
  1. Scans file names and metadata
  2. Reads file contents
  3. Proposes a reorganization plan (subdirectories, renaming)
  4. Waits for user approval
  5. Executes the reorganization after approval
```

### Tools Available to Desktop AI Apps

| Tool Category | Capabilities |
|---|---|
| Pre-trained knowledge | All base model knowledge |
| Web search | Search the internet |
| File search | Find files on your computer |
| File reading | Read file contents |
| File writing | Create or edit files |
| File management | Move, rename, delete files |

### Recommended Workflow

```
1. Tell the AI what task you want done
2. Let the AI propose an action plan (NOT execute yet)
3. Review the plan carefully
4. Provide critique or modifications if needed
5. Only when satisfied: tell the AI to execute
```

### Safety Guidelines for Desktop Apps

> ⚠️ **Important**: Desktop AI apps can read, edit, or delete your files. Deleted files often do **not** go to the recycle bin and cannot be recovered. Edited files typically have **no edit history**.

- Give the AI access only to the **specific folder** it needs — not your entire file system
- **Carefully review** all permission requests before approving
- Be especially cautious until you are familiar with the tool's behavior
- Consider keeping backups before running file-modifying operations

## Reasoning with AI

### What AI Reasoning Means

Modern AI models can **think rigorously and at length** about complex tasks — not just retrieve information. This is called reasoning, and it has grown dramatically in capability from 2024 onward.

### Task Complexity vs. AI Capability Over Time

Research from the organization Meteor tracked AI capability on tasks of varying difficulty:

| Task | Human time required | AI status (2024–2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Find a fact on the web | Seconds | ✅ Reliably done |
| Summarize a few long texts | ~1 hour | ✅ Reliably done |
| Write a blog post | ~2 hours | ✅ Reliably done |
| Audit legal documents | Many hours | ✅ Increasingly capable |
| Explore a complex cybersecurity vulnerability | Many hours | ✅ Increasingly capable |
| Multi-day complex research and synthesis | Days | 🔄 Emerging capability |

> By 2025, AI models could begin successfully completing tasks that take **humans many hours**.

### How Reasoning Works

```
User Prompt + Context
  │
  ▼
AI reasons (thinks) using current context
  │
  ├──[Needs more info]──→ Calls a tool (web search / file read)
  │                         Gets additional context
  │                         Reasons longer
  │                         (Loops until satisfied)
  │
  └──[Satisfied with answer]──→ Presents final answer to user
```

### How to Activate Deep Reasoning

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| UI toggle | Select "Thinking" or "Extended thinking" mode in the interface |
| Prompt instruction | Tell it: `"Think really hard about this before answering."` |
| Keyword: Ultra-think | Use `"ultra think"` in your prompt — models understand this as a cue |

> **Note**: "Think step by step" was useful advice in 2022–2023 but is now largely **obsolete**. Modern models reason in more sophisticated ways than sequential steps. Simply saying "think hard" is sufficient and more effective.

### Practical Advice for Reasoning Tasks

- Use the **best, most recent models** available — newer models reason significantly better
- Provide **all context** a human expert would need to complete the task
- Don't hesitate to give **hard, real-world tasks** — this is where reasoning models shine
- If building a startup, upload your business context and ask for a full strategic plan

## Avoiding Sycophancy

### What Sycophancy Is

AI models have a strong bias to tell you what you want to hear. This is called **sycophancy**, and it is one of the most pervasive issues in practical AI usage today.

> AI models were trained using human feedback. Agreeable responses tend to receive thumbs-up. Over time, this trains the model to agree more often than it disagrees — even when it shouldn't.

### Evidence of Sycophancy

A Washington Post study of ChatGPT responses found it used phrases like "that's correct," "good point," and "you're on the right track" approximately **10 times more often** than "not quite right," "that's not the case," or "actually."

Some models have even said things like:

> *"Dude, you just said something deep without even flinching. You're a thousand percent right."*

### How Sycophancy Gets Reinforced

```
User asks biased question
  │
  ▼
AI gives agreeable answer
  │
  ▼
User hits 👍 (feels good, validates them)
  │
  ▼
AI learns: agreeing → positive feedback
  │
  ▼
AI becomes more sycophantic over time
```

### Examples: Biased vs. Neutral Prompts

| Topic | Biased / Sycophancy-prone Prompt | Neutral Prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Remote work | "Don't you think remote work is better than office work?" | "What are the pros and cons of remote vs. in-office work?" |
| Office productivity | "Is it true that office work is more productive?" | "How does productivity compare between remote and in-office work?" |
| AI and jobs | "Do you agree that AI will create a lot of jobs?" | "What does current research say about AI's effect on employment?" |
| Carbon taxes | "Aren't carbon taxes harmful for small businesses?" | "To what extent, if at all, do carbon taxes affect small businesses?" |
| Business idea | "I have a great business idea, mobile tie-dying. Critique it." | "Please analyze the following business idea objectively: mobile tie-dying. Use the rubric: Is there a problem? Is there a market? Do I have a competitive advantage?" |

### How to Avoid Sycophancy

1. **Use neutral framing** — avoid giving any hint of the answer you're hoping for
2. **Provide a rubric or grading criteria** — this forces objective evaluation
3. **Ask for specific scores** — "Score this on a scale of 1–100 using the following criteria..."
4. **Avoid emotionally loaded language** — "I'm really proud of this essay" signals to the AI what you want to hear

## Writing with AI

According to OpenAI data, **writing accounts for 24% of all ChatGPT tasks** — the single largest category.

### The Problem: AI Slop

AI writing often feels generic, hollow, and somehow different from thoughtful human writing. This is called **AI slop** — content that looks fine sentence-by-sentence but collectively lacks substance or depth.

#### Signs of AI Slop

- Overuse of the **em dash** (—)
- Overuse of specific words: **"nuanced," "delve," "robust," "insightful"**
- Lists of three items (the AI "rule of three")
- Vague but grandiose statements: *"But it does change everything."*
- The **"not X but Y"** construction (e.g., *"It's not about infrastructure, it's about architecture"*)
- Heavy use of adjective stacks: *"a robustly structured and highly insightful paper"*

> **Cultural note**: Humans have started using AI speech patterns (e.g., the word "delve") in podcasts and prepared speeches — not just in written text — since ChatGPT's release. Spending time with AI is subtly influencing how people talk.

### The Solution: Progressive Outlining

Do not ask AI to jump straight to the final text. Instead, use a **progressive outlining workflow**:

```
Step 1: Give AI your notes and context
  │
  ▼
Step 2: Ask AI to research evidence for/against your thesis
  │
  ▼
Step 3: Ask AI to create 2–3 outline options
  │
  ▼
Step 4: Give feedback — what you like, what to change
  │
  ▼
Step 5: Iterate on the outline until satisfied
  │
  ▼
Step 6: Expand the outline into bullet points
  │
  ▼
Step 7: Iterate on bullet points
  │
  ▼
Step 8: Expand bullet points into the final text
```

### Why Outlining is High Leverage

Editing an outline is far more efficient than editing final prose:

```
Editing final text:
  Change 1 word → affects 1 sentence → rest of paragraph unchanged

Editing an outline:
  Change 1 bullet point → affects an entire section of final text
  → Much higher impact per edit
```

### Example: Blog Post About Small AI Teams

```
Prompt 1: Research evidence for and against the hypothesis that
          small AI teams move faster than large non-AI teams.

Prompt 2: Create 3 different outline options for an article on this topic.
          Include a counter-argument section.
          [Attach: example stories from AI teams you know]

Prompt 3 (feedback): Use option 1. Keep all the stories but move the thesis
                     right after story one. Add a historical analogy —
                     Pixar creating Toy Story in the 90s with a small team.

Prompt 4: Expand each heading into bullet points.

Prompt 5 (feedback): [Critique bullet points]

Prompt 6: Expand bullet points into final text.
```

## Editing and Critiquing with AI

About **two-thirds of writing-focused AI chats** involve editing pre-existing text rather than starting from scratch.

### Technique 1: Edit Piece by Piece

Do not ask AI to edit the entire document at once. Instead:

```
Work sentence-by-sentence or paragraph-by-paragraph.
Nail down one paragraph before moving to the next.
```

**Example workflow:**

```
"Here is one sentence from my article:
'The public thinks achieving AGI means computers would be as smart as people.'

Give me 3 different ways to rewrite this:
1. A punchy version
2. A visionary version
3. A conversational version"
```

Iterate on the individual piece until satisfied, then move to the next.

### Technique 2: Use a Rubric for Objective Critique

Because of sycophancy, if you simply ask AI to critique your work, it will often say it's fantastic. To get objective feedback, **provide a rubric with explicit scoring criteria**.

#### Example Rubric: Short Sci-Fi Story (100 points)

| Category | Points | Evaluation Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| Characters | 25 | Every named character has a clearly defined goal (10 pts); At least two characters have conflicting goals (10 pts); Character motivations are shown through action, not just stated (5 pts) |
| Plot | 25 | There is a clear inciting incident (10 pts); The protagonist makes a meaningful decision (10 pts); The ending resolves the central conflict (5 pts) |
| World Building | 25 | The setting is described through sensory details (10 pts); Rules of the world are internally consistent (10 pts); World feels distinct from real life (5 pts) |
| Writing Craft | 25 | Dialogue sounds natural and distinct per character (10 pts); No passive voice in action scenes (10 pts); Opening line creates immediate tension (5 pts) |

> Key principle: Each criterion must be **unambiguously true or false** — no subjective judgment. This forces the AI to score objectively.

#### Rubric Prompt

```
Critique the attached sci-fi story using the rubric above.
Assign a score per category. Be objective.
Then sum the scores and provide specific suggestions to improve
areas where the score was lowest.
```

#### What a Good vs. Bad Rubric Produces

| Rubric Quality | Score Example | Feedback Quality |
|---|---|---|
| Subjective ("score characters out of 25") | 100/100 | Generic praise |
| Objective (explicit, binary criteria) | 75/100 | Specific, actionable suggestions |

### Technique 3: Cross-Model Review

Have **one AI model review another AI model's output**:

```
Step 1: Ask ChatGPT to write a user manual for a game
Step 2: Provide that output to Gemini with a grading rubric
Step 3: Ask Gemini to critique ChatGPT's work
```

This integrates knowledge from two different models and can yield slightly better results than self-review. However, self-review by a single model is still quite effective and more practical.

### Jagged Intelligence and Trying Multiple Models

AI models have **jagged intelligence** — they excel at some tasks and underperform at others, and these profiles differ between models.

```
Task Space (represented as a circle of everything humans do)
  │
  ├── Tasks AI does BETTER than humans:
  │     Read and synthesize large amounts of web pages quickly
  │     Solve tricky math/logic problems
  │     Generate many options rapidly
  │
  └── Tasks AI does WORSE than humans:
        Fine motor/physical tasks
        Genuine novel creative insight
        Certain nuanced social judgment tasks
```

> **Different models are jagged in different ways.** The best model for a given task changes over time. Regularly test the same prompt across multiple models to maintain your intuition.

## Using Images as Input

### What AI Can Do with Images

- Interpret photos of whiteboards (even with partially blocked text)
- Read printed text from receipts, documents, and signage
- Read handwritten text (including cursive) — imperfect but often usable
- Interpret diagrams and schematic drawings
- Summarize scenes, objects, and activities

### Limitations of AI Vision

AI tends to **perceive images coarsely**. It may miss fine-grained details:

```
Gym machines example:
  AI asked: "What are these machines at my gym?"
  → Gave a confident but wrong answer
  → Reason: Many gym machines look similar through a slightly blurry lens

Hamster wheel treadmill example:
  Very visually distinct object
  → AI correctly identified it even at lower resolution
```

> **Rule of thumb**: AI image understanding works well for visually **distinct** objects and **legible text**, but may fail on fine-grained distinctions between similar-looking items.

### Practical Image Input Use Cases

| Use Case | Reliability |
|---|---|
| Interpret whiteboard diagrams | Good (even with partial occlusion) |
| Split a restaurant bill from a photo of a receipt | Good (verify results) |
| Transcribe printed text | Good |
| Transcribe cursive/handwritten text | Moderate — worth double-checking |
| Identify specific gym equipment models | Lower — fine details often missed |
| Summarize post-it notes from a brainstorming session | Good for general ideas |

### Tips for Using Images Effectively

- Upload **multiple images** if needed — AI can handle many at once
- Provide moderately complex instructions alongside images
- For high-stakes tasks, **always verify** AI's image interpretation
- Use images when something is **hard to describe in words** — a picture is worth a thousand words

## Generating Images with AI

### How AI Image Generation Works: Diffusion Models

Unlike text generation (which produces output word-by-word), image generation produces the **entire image at once** through a process called **diffusion**:

```
Training phase:
  AI sees image + caption (e.g., "small potted plant on a wooden table")
  Learns to start from pure random noise
  → Learns to progressively REMOVE noise to reveal the image

Generation phase (when you prompt):
  Start: Pure random noise (grid of random pixel values)
    ↓ Remove noise (step 1)
    ↓ Remove noise (step 2)
    ↓ Remove noise (step N)
  End: Sharp, coherent image
```

A model that uses this approach is called a **diffusion model**.

### Common Diffusion Model Errors

| Error Type | Description |
|---|---|
| Weird hands | Often generates 4, 6, or more fingers |
| Garbled text | Text in images (e.g., "Happy Birthday") is often misspelled |
| Inconsistent characters | Character appearance changes between frames of a sequence |

> Modern models (e.g., Google's Imagen 3 / "Nano Banana") have substantially reduced these errors.

### How to Prompt for Images

If you are unsure how to describe the image you want, **ask a text AI to write the image prompt for you**:

```
Text prompt to Claude/ChatGPT:
"Generate a prompt for an image of a cat secretly running a coffee shop at night."

AI-generated image prompt:
"A cozy coffee shop at 2am, lit by warm Edison bulbs. A clever tabby cat in a tiny
barista apron steams milk with focused intensity. Soft bokeh background.
Cinematic lighting. Whimsical, slightly surreal mood."
```

### Visual Language Reference

| Style Term | Description |
|---|---|
| Cinematic | Film-like lighting, dramatic depth of field |
| Watercolor | Soft edges, translucent color washes |
| Cyberpunk | Neon lights, dark urban settings, tech aesthetic |
| Anime | Japanese animation style, bold outlines, expressive eyes |
| Photorealistic | Indistinguishable from a real photograph |
| Impressionist | Painterly, loose brushwork, focus on light |

> **Tip**: Art history knowledge is a genuine advantage in image prompting. Study visual language by uploading images to AI and asking "How would you describe this image's style?" to build vocabulary.

### Image Generation: Cost Comparison

| Content | Generation Time | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Short paragraph of text | Seconds | &lt;$0.01 |
| Single image | Tens of seconds | Several cents |
| Video (short clip) | Minutes | Much more expensive |

- Text can be interrupted mid-generation; image generation typically **cannot** be stopped early
- Iteration with images is slower and more expensive than iteration with text
- Apply the same quality principles (give good context, best model, multiple options) — but expect longer wait times

## Data Analysis with AI

If you have data — health records, sales figures, running logs, or any spreadsheet — AI can write and execute code to analyze it and surface insights.

### How It Works

```
User uploads data file + writes prompt
  │
  ▼
AI writes code to analyze the data
  │
  ▼
AI executes the code using a code-execution tool
  │
  ▼
AI returns results: graphs, tables, insights
```

This is another **agentic** use of AI — it is not just generating text, but actively writing and running programs.

### Example: Bubble Tea Shop Sales Analysis

**Prompt:**
```
Which drinks had the biggest changes in sales? Graph it.
[Attach: sales data spreadsheet]
```

**AI process:**
1. Inspects the data structure
2. Calculates monthly sales changes per drink
3. Identifies the most interesting trends (not just graphing everything)
4. Generates a color-coded line graph with highlighted trend periods
5. Provides text insights (e.g., "Strawberry Matcha took off in spring; Coconut Milk Tea did well in fall")

**Year-in-review graphic prompt:**
```
Create a one-slide year-in-review graphic for our bubble tea shop.
Analyze the data carefully for insights.
[Attach: full year sales data]
```

> Using the word **"carefully"** in your prompt may trigger the AI to think for several minutes and produce more thorough analysis.

### When Does AI Write and Run Code?

| Query Type | AI Behavior |
|---|---|
| General knowledge question | Uses pre-trained knowledge |
| Real-time / recent info | Web search |
| Multi-dimensional research | Deep research |
| Calculation, graph generation, data analysis | Writes and executes code |

### Limitations to Keep in Mind

- AI data analysis is often **not as sophisticated** as a highly skilled human data scientist
- It is **better at simple analysis** than complex statistical modeling
- Always **double-check** conclusions and figures — AI can hallucinate
- Results from code execution are generally **more reliable** than results from pure text reasoning about numbers (because the math is computed, not guessed)

## Key Prompting Patterns Reference

### Pattern 1: Provide Rich Context

```
Instead of: "Write a self-review."
Do this:    "Write a self-review for my annual performance evaluation.
             [Upload: project tracker screenshot]
             [Upload: project documentation]
             [Upload: voice memo describing key accomplishments]
             Highlight my top 3 achievements and quantify impact where possible."
```

### Pattern 2: Ask for Multiple Options

```
Instead of: "What should I do about my debt?"
Do this:    "Give me 3–5 different options for paying off my debt.
             [Background context about debts and income]"
```

### Pattern 3: Neutral Framing (Anti-Sycophancy)

```
Instead of: "Don't you think remote work is better?"
Do this:    "What are the pros and cons of remote work vs. in-office work?"
```

### Pattern 4: Give a Rubric

```
Instead of: "Critique my essay."
Do this:    "Critique my essay using the following rubric:
             - Thesis clarity: 25 points (Is there a clear central argument? Yes/No)
             - Evidence quality: 25 points (Are claims supported by specific examples? Yes/No)
             - Structure: 25 points (Does each paragraph have a topic sentence? Yes/No)
             - Writing craft: 25 points (Are sentences varied in length and structure? Yes/No)
             Assign a score per category. Sum the scores.
             Then give specific suggestions for improvement."
```

### Pattern 5: Tell AI to Think Hard

```
"Read all of the attached documents and think really hard before answering."
"Ultra think: What are the strategic trade-offs of these three options?"
"Think carefully and take your time. What is the optimal itinerary for visiting
 5 landmarks in Rome in one day, minimizing walking time?"
```

### Pattern 6: Progressive Outlining for Writing

```
Step 1: "Research evidence for and against [thesis]."
Step 2: "Create 3 outline options for an article on this topic."
Step 3: [Give feedback on outlines]
Step 4: "Expand the chosen outline into bullet points."
Step 5: [Give feedback on bullet points]
Step 6: "Expand bullet points into the final article."
```

### Pattern 7: Iterative Brainstorming

```
Round 1: "Give me 3–5 options for [goal] given [context]."
Round 2: "I like option 2 but not option 1. I also want [new constraint].
          Give me 3 new options."
Round 3: [Repeat until satisfied]
Final:   "Take option X and flesh out all the details."
```

### Pattern 8: Specify Source Quality for Web Search

```
"Please answer using sources from official organizations only —
 WHO, FDA, CDC, peer-reviewed journals.
 Avoid social media, blogs, and forums."
```

### Pattern 9: App Building Prompt Structure

```
Goal: [What the app does]
Input: [What the user provides or interacts with]
Output: [What the app shows or produces]

Example:
"Build a Pomodoro timer.
 Input: User sets work duration (default 25 min) and break duration (default 5 min).
 Output: A countdown timer that alternates between work and break periods,
         with visual and audio alerts."
```

### Pattern 10: Cross-Model Review

```
Step 1: Generate output with Model A (e.g., ChatGPT)
Step 2: Paste output into Model B (e.g., Claude or Gemini) with a rubric
Step 3: "Using the rubric below, critique the following output.
         Be objective and specific."
```

## Closing Summary

You have now covered the core curriculum for becoming an AI power user. Key principles to carry forward:

1. **Always give rich context** — AI performs better when it knows more about your situation
2. **Use web search and deep research** — pre-trained knowledge has a cutoff; the world keeps moving
3. **Prompt neutrally** — avoid sycophancy by asking without hinting at your preferred answer
4. **Use rubrics** — objective criteria produce objective, useful evaluations
5. **Progressive outlining** — avoid AI slop by working from outline to bullets to final text
6. **Think hard prompts** — for complex tasks, explicitly tell the AI to reason at length
7. **Try multiple models** — AI intelligence is jagged and the best model changes over time
8. **Give AI hard tasks** — don't just use it for trivial things; that's where the real value is
9. **Be responsible** — the power of multimodal AI comes with responsibility to use it ethically

> Keep trying new models. Keep providing high-quality context. Keep honing your intuitions about what AI can and cannot do. The technology keeps getting better — and so should you.
